# Chapter One

*(draft 3 — novel, working title THE KEEPER'S LEDGER. ~1,700 words. Writing group feedback due the 9th)*

The supply boat left Ines Varga on the dock at Corran Rock with four crates of tinned food, two drums of diesel, and a man named Albrecht who did not stop talking for the entire forty minutes it took to unload.

"You'll want to check the generator first thing every morning," he said, heaving a crate onto the concrete. "Before coffee. Before anything. She's temperamental, but she'll tell you when she's sulking if you listen. And the radio — channel sixteen, always on, even at night. Especially at night." He paused, looked up at the tower, and then at her. "They told you about Mattis?"

"They told me the previous keeper retired."

Albrecht made a noise that was not quite a laugh. "Retired. Yes. That's a word for it." He lifted the last drum, set it down, and wiped his palms on his trousers. "Six weeks, the boat comes. If you need it sooner, you call. Don't be proud about calling."

Then he was gone, the boat's engine fading into the gray chop of the strait, and Ines was alone on a rock half a mile long and a few hundred yards wide, with a lighthouse, a cottage, a shed, and roughly ten thousand seabirds who did not appear pleased to see her.

---

She had applied for the job on a night in March, sitting on the floor of her apartment in the city among boxes she had not yet unpacked and would now never need to. The advertisement had been small and oddly worded — *Seeking reliable person for a position of solitude. Mechanical aptitude preferred. Must be comfortable with weather.* — and she had laughed when she read it, then read it again, then filled in the form before she could talk herself out of it. She had been comfortable with nothing for a long time. Weather seemed manageable.

The light itself was automated now; it had been for twenty years. A keeper was not strictly necessary. But the maritime authority kept one on Corran Rock anyway, partly for the weather reports, partly to maintain the backup systems, and partly, the woman who interviewed her had admitted, because of tradition. "It's one of the last ones," she'd said. "When it goes, it goes. Nobody wants to be the one who signs that order."

Ines carried the crates up the steep path to the cottage two at a time and was soaked with sweat by the end, despite the wind. The cottage was small and smelled of salt and kerosene. There was a narrow bed, a woodstove, a table with one chair, and a shelf of paperbacks with swollen spines. On the table was a thick ledger bound in green cloth, its corners worn white.

She opened it. The first entries were from before she was born, written in a careful, upright hand: wind direction, barometric pressure, visibility, vessels sighted. *14 Nov. Wind NW force 6. Pressure falling. Visibility 2 nm. Fishing vessel KESTREL passed 0700, northbound.* Page after page, year after year, the handwriting changing every decade or so as one keeper replaced another. She turned forward to the last pages, where the writing became cramped and slanted, as if written in a hurry or in poor light.

The final entry was dated nine months ago.

*2 Feb. Wind S force 8 gusting 9. Pressure 971 and falling. Visibility nil. Radio contact with vessel MARGARETHE lost 2210. Signal was clear, then nothing. She was eleven miles out and making for the strait. Light functioning. I kept the light. I kept the light.*

Below that, in the same hand, but steadier, as though written on a different day:

*They say there was no vessel by that name registered in the strait that night. They say I was tired. I know what I heard.*

And then nothing. Blank pages to the end of the book.

Ines closed the ledger and sat with her hand on its cover for a long moment while the wind pushed at the window. Then she got up, found the kettle, and filled it from the rainwater tap, because it was something to do with her hands.

---

The first week passed the way she imagined first weeks passed everywhere: in learning. She learned that the generator did sulk, exactly as Albrecht had said, and that the sulking could usually be cured by bleeding air from the fuel line and speaking to it in a low, reasonable voice. She learned that the birds — guillemots, mostly, and a colony of kittiwakes on the north cliff — woke at four in the morning and did not believe in quiet. She learned the sixty-three steps of the tower by feel, and how to climb them in the dark without a torch, one hand on the cold iron rail.

She learned the routine of the weather reports. Every three hours, she walked out to the instrument enclosure, recorded the readings, and called them in on the radio to the coastguard station on the mainland. The voice on the other end was usually a young man named Teodor who said "Copy that, Corran Rock," in a tone of great seriousness, and once, on the fourth night, added, "Hope you're settling in all right," and then cleared his throat as if embarrassed to have said it.

"I am," she said. "Thank you."

She did not tell him about the ledger.

She did not tell anyone that on the fifth night, as she sat by the stove with the radio murmuring static on channel sixteen, she thought she heard, for just a second, a voice beneath the hiss. A woman's voice, faint and clipped, saying something that might have been a call sign. When she turned the volume up there was only static, and the wind, and the distant mechanical sigh of the light turning above her.

She wrote in the ledger that night for the first time. Her handwriting looked strange under all those others, too round, too new.

*9 Nov. Wind SW force 5. Pressure 1008, steady. Visibility 8 nm. No vessels sighted. Possible voice on ch. 16 at 2340, not repeated. Probably interference.*

She looked at the word *probably* for a long time before she went to bed.

The next morning the fog came in so thick that she could not see the shed from the cottage door. She did the generator check by touch and by sound, kneeling on the cold floor of the engine room while the machine coughed and caught and settled into its steady drone. On the way back she stopped halfway up the path and stood perfectly still, listening. The foghorn on the mainland was sounding its long, low note every thirty seconds, and between the notes there was nothing at all — not the birds, not the sea, not even the wind. It was the quietest place she had ever been. She found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid of it. She had spent years in rooms full of people where she felt more alone than this.

She spent the afternoon reading the old ledger from the beginning, the way someone else might read a novel. The keepers rarely wrote anything personal. But here and there, a line slipped through: *Wife arrived on supply boat, stayed three days. Good days.* Or: *Fog all week. Have begun talking to the gulls. They do not talk back, which is an improvement on some people.* She laughed out loud at that one, and the sound startled her.

---

On the eighth day, she found the box.

She had been clearing out the shed, which was full of the debris of decades: coils of rope, paint cans fused shut, a broken chair, a crate of glass lamp chimneys from the days before electricity. Behind a stack of oilskins at the back, she found a tin box, the kind that had once held biscuits, with a picture of a sailing ship on the lid. Inside were letters. Dozens of them, bundled with string, all addressed to the same name — *Mattis Kroll, Keeper, Corran Rock Light* — and all, as far as she could tell, unopened.

No. Not unopened. She held one up to the light from the doorway. The envelopes had been slit neatly along the top and then pressed closed again, carefully, as though whoever had read them wanted to pretend they hadn't.

The return address on every one of them was the same: a street in a coastal town she'd never heard of, and a name — *M. Lindqvist*.

The postmarks began twelve years ago and ended in January, a month before the last entry in the ledger.

Ines sat down on an upturned bucket in the doorway of the shed with the tin box in her lap. Out over the water, the light was not yet on, but the sky was beginning to dim, the clouds going lavender at their edges. Somewhere below her, the sea was doing what it always did — moving, endlessly, without any interest in her or in the man who had kept this light before her and had heard a ship that was not there.

She untied the string.
